00 · History & paradigms

How we got here

AI is not a single invention. It is a long series of ideas, winters, and engineering breakthroughs.

Timeline

Brief historical arc

1950s–60s: Turing’s test, Dartmouth workshop, early symbolic programs and search. 1970s–80s: expert systems, knowledge representation; then funding winters. 1990s–2000s: statistical machine learning, SVMs, probabilistic models, early neural nets. 2010s: deep learning renaissance (ImageNet, AlexNet), GPUs, large datasets. 2017–: transformers, scaling laws, foundation models, multimodal systems, tool-using agents.

Progress has been uneven. Many ideas recycled; compute, data, and engineering often mattered as much as pure theory.

Paradigms

Symbolic, connectionist, hybrid

Symbolic AI: explicit rules, logic, search over structured representations. Strong on explainability when the knowledge is correct; brittle when the world is messy.

Connectionist / neural: learned distributed representations and pattern recognition from data. Dominant in current large models; less transparent by default.

Hybrid approaches: combine neural perception with tools, planners, knowledge bases, or verifiers. Many practical systems already do this (RAG, code interpreters, search).

Learning styles

Major ML families

Supervised: labeled input→output pairs. Unsupervised / self-supervised: structure from the data itself (next-token prediction is a famous example). Reinforcement learning: learn from rewards and interaction. Imitation / preference learning: learn from demonstrations or ranked outputs. Modern pipelines mix several of these stages.

Today

What “AI” usually means now

In public conversation, “AI” often means large language or multimodal models that generate fluent text, images, code, or speech. Technically, AI still includes classical search, optimization, control systems, computer vision pipelines, recommender systems, and robotics stacks. Always ask which system and which task.

01 · Foundations

What counts as an AI model?

Separate the model, the data, the training algorithm, and the deployed system.

Model

A learned function

A model is a mathematical object that maps inputs to outputs. In a neural network, learned parameters combine with architecture and operations to compute that mapping.

y = fθ(x)

θ denotes parameters; x the input; y the computed output. The equation hides a lot: tokenization, layers, normalization, attention, output heads and decoding.

System

More than the neural network

A deployed AI product may include a model, system instructions, filters, retrieval index, tools, routing, caches, monitoring, user interface and policy logic. Its behavior cannot always be attributed to the weights alone.

Different users can see different behavior because of context, model version, settings, tools, or safety systems.

Families

Major model types

Discriminative: classify or estimate labels. Generative: model a data distribution and create samples. Embedding: map items to vectors. Reinforcement learning: learn policies from reward. Multimodal: process or generate more than one modality.

Examples include CNNs, transformers, diffusion models, recurrent networks, graph neural networks, state-space models and hybrid systems.

Intelligence

Capability is not consciousness

Fluent language, planning-like behavior or a high benchmark score does not by itself establish subjective experience, understanding in a human sense, or consciousness. These are separate empirical and philosophical questions.

Measure specific abilities with defined tests rather than treating “intelligence” as one simple dial.

02 · Architecture

How neural networks are assembled

Architecture specifies the operations and connectivity; training determines parameter values.

Transformer anatomy

Many language models use transformer blocks. A simplified block combines attention and a feed-forward network, with residual paths and normalization.

InputToken embeddings + positions
→
AttentionMix information across positions
→
MLPNonlinear feature transformation
Residual streamInformation carried through layers
→
Output headLogits / predictions

This is conceptual: exact ordering, normalization, attention variants, and routing differ by model.

Attention, in a nutshell

Queries compare with keys to create weights that mix value vectors. This lets each position use relevant information from other positions.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ)V

Multi-head attention learns multiple projection spaces. Causal masks prevent a next-token model from looking at future tokens during training.

Costs: standard dense attention has quadratic sequence-length work/memory characteristics in its basic form. Flash-style kernels, sparse patterns, grouped-query attention and other techniques improve practical efficiency.

Other architecture families

CNNs: local filters and spatial structure. RNN/LSTM: recurrent state. Diffusion: iteratively denoise samples. GNNs: pass messages over graph connections. State-space models: efficient sequence dynamics. Mixture of experts: route tokens through selected subnetworks.

Hybrid and multimodal systems may combine several families rather than fitting one label.

Representations: tokens, embeddings, features

A tokenizer maps text to discrete IDs. An embedding table maps IDs to vectors. Hidden states evolve through layers into contextual representations. Image systems may use patches or learned visual tokens; audio systems may use waveforms, spectrograms, codecs or discrete audio tokens.

Representations are learned and distributed. A single neuron rarely corresponds cleanly to one human-readable concept in every context.

03 · Weights & tensors

Inside the parameter space

Explore what weights are, how parameter count translates to storage, and why weights are not a readable list of facts.

Parameter storage calculator

Choose a hypothetical model size and numeric precision. This estimates raw parameter storage only—not the full memory required to run the model.

Decimal GB uses 1 GB = 10⁹ bytes. Actual files include metadata and quantization scales; runtime memory also includes activations, temporary buffers and KV cache.

What is a weight?

A weight is a learned numerical parameter, commonly part of a matrix or tensor. It contributes to transformations such as a linear projection: h = Wx + b.

Weights are not typically hand-written rules. Training adjusts many parameters together so the network's predictions better fit an objective. Knowledge and behaviors are distributed across parameter interactions, activations, data exposure and system setup.

Biases are additional learned offsets. Activations are intermediate values computed for a particular input; they are not the same thing as weights.

Quantization

Quantization represents parameters or activations with fewer bits, often reducing memory and improving throughput. It introduces approximation error and the quality impact depends on the model, quantizer, calibration data and task.

FP32, BF16, FP16, INT8, 4-bit formats and mixed precision are not interchangeable labels. “4-bit model” does not mean every part of the runtime uses exactly four bits.

Training memory vs inference memory

Training stores gradients and optimizer states in addition to parameters, and often retains activations for backpropagation. Inference can avoid optimizer states but still needs working memory and, for autoregressive transformers, a KV cache.

Sharding, checkpointing, offloading, low-rank adaptation and optimizer choices can substantially alter the real hardware requirement.

04 · Teaching & training

How models learn

“Teaching” usually means constructing data and objectives that shape a model’s behavior—not explaining concepts to a human brain.

3. PredictForward pass
4. ScoreCompute loss
→
5. DifferentiateBackpropagate gradients
→
6. UpdateOptimizer changes parameters

Repeat over batches; validate on held-out data; monitor failures; select checkpoints; test before deployment. Large training jobs require distributed systems, numerical stability and fault recovery.

Core learning paradigms

Supervised learning: learn from examples paired with labels or target outputs. Self-supervised learning: create training targets from the data itself, such as predicting masked or next tokens. Reinforcement learning: update a policy using reward signals. Contrastive learning: bring related representations together and separate mismatches.

Modern foundation-model pipelines may combine pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, reinforcement learning, synthetic data and domain adaptation.

Loss, gradients and optimization

A loss function measures model error relative to a training target. Backpropagation applies the chain rule to calculate parameter gradients. An optimizer uses those gradients to update parameters.

θ ← θ − η ∇θL(θ)

Here η is the learning rate. SGD, momentum and Adam-family optimizers are common examples. Training is sensitive to learning rate, batch size, data order, initialization, numerical precision and many other choices.

Alignment and “instruction following”

After broad pretraining, a model may be fine-tuned on demonstrations and preferences to better follow instructions. Human or AI feedback can help rank outputs; algorithms then optimize toward the chosen signal.

Feedback is an imperfect proxy for what people truly want. It can reward confident style over correctness, reflect rater bias, or leave gaps in rare situations. Safety tuning reduces some risks but cannot guarantee harmless or truthful behavior.

Training playground: change the learning rate

This is a conceptual optimizer demonstration, not a training run. A single parameter tries to approach a target value. Very high learning rates can overshoot; small ones move slowly.

Parameter starts at 0. Target is 1.0. Loss is squared error.
05 · Inference & context

From prompt to output

Generation is a sequence of computed choices, with randomness and constraints depending on decoding settings.

Autoregressive generation

A next-token language model computes scores (logits), converts them into a probability distribution, selects a token under a decoding rule, appends it to context and repeats until a stopping condition.

PromptExisting tokens
→
Model passScores next token
→
DecodeChoose one token
↺

“Thinking time” in a chat interface can mean extra generated tokens, tool calls, search, internal routing or compute allocation. It is not a direct meter of consciousness.

Change temperature and top-k in a toy distribution. This uses invented sample logits to demonstrate the mechanics, not a live model.

Context window and memory

The context window is the amount of input/output token context the model can process in a call. Long context does not guarantee perfect recall, equal attention to every position, or unlimited persistent memory.

Products may store conversation history separately, retrieve relevant documents, or summarize old messages. That external memory is distinct from the model's trained weights.

Tools, retrieval and agents

A tool-using system may decide to call a calculator, code runner, database, browser or another model. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fetches relevant material and places it in context. An agent loop may plan, act, observe and repeat.

Tools can improve grounding, but introduce permissions, latency, prompt-injection, data-exfiltration and error-propagation risks. Tool output must be validated rather than blindly trusted.

06 · How AI “thinks”

What happens inside a model?

Use precise language: computation and learned representations are observable; human-like inner experience is not established by fluent output.

A scientific account of “thinking”

During inference, input is transformed into vectors; layers repeatedly mix and transform these representations; output scores are produced. Some models are trained to generate intermediate reasoning steps or use explicit search and tools. These mechanisms can support multi-step problem solving.

Researchers probe internal representations, attention patterns, activation features, circuits and causal interventions. Interpretability is an active field, and current explanations are incomplete. Attention maps alone are not a full explanation of a model’s decision.

Why models can look as if they reason

Training data contains patterns of explanation, mathematics, code and problem-solving. Optimization can produce internal computation that generalizes beyond memorized examples. Additional inference-time computation, decomposition, verification and tool use can improve some tasks.

However, a plausible explanation can be post-hoc, incomplete or wrong. A model may solve a task by shortcuts, fail under small changes, or confidently fabricate information.

What we can inspect

Inputs and outputs: prompts, completions, token scores when exposed. Parameters: tensors if the model is open and access is authorized. Activations: intermediate vectors. Gradients: sensitivity during training. Traces: tool calls and observable steps. Benchmarks: external task performance.

Closed APIs usually do not expose raw weights, all activations, training data, or private internal traces. Never assume an interface reveals the complete internal process.

Human brain comparison: useful but limited

Both brains and neural networks involve many interacting units, but artificial networks use engineered numerical operations and optimization procedures. The name “neural network” is inspired by neuroscience, not proof that the system operates like a biological brain.

Brains are embodied biological systems with development, metabolism, sensory-motor loops and evolutionary history. Comparing parameter counts to neuron counts does not establish equivalence.

07 · Code laboratory

Minimal neural-network mathematics

Small, transparent examples show the shape of the work—not the complete code of a production foundation model.

Examples are illustrative pseudocode / Python-like snippets.


Open-source model workflow
Typical workflow: inspect the model card and license; verify provenance; load tokenizer and weights; run a small test; benchmark quality and latency; measure memory; evaluate security; pin versions; monitor production drift.
Never load untrusted serialized model files or custom code without a security review. Model artifacts can execute code depending on format and loader settings.


Fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompt design
Prompt design: changes the instructions and examples at runtime. RAG: retrieves current or private knowledge into context. Fine-tuning: changes model parameters to alter behavior or task adaptation. Training from scratch: learns a broad model from an initial parameter state and requires far more data and compute.
Choose based on the problem: current facts often suit retrieval; stable output format or domain behavior may suit fine-tuning; simple task framing may need only a prompt.



08 · Evaluation
How to know whether a model works
One benchmark score cannot summarize all capability, reliability, fairness or safety.


Evaluation dimensions
AccuracyCalibrationRobustnessGeneralizationLatencyCostPrivacySecurityFairnessReliability
Use representative held-out data, adversarial cases, realistic workflows and human review. Track uncertainty and failure severity, not just average score.


Benchmark pitfalls
Data contamination can inflate results. A model may overfit benchmark formats, benefit from tool access, or perform differently across languages and user groups. Multiple-choice scores may not reflect open-ended reliability.
Report the exact model/version, prompt template, tools, sampling settings, sample size, confidence intervals and evaluation date.


Hallucination and calibration
Generative models can produce plausible false claims because next-token plausibility is not the same as verified truth. Calibration asks whether confidence matches actual correctness. Retrieval and citations help only if sources are relevant and claims are checked against them.
Good systems can abstain, ask clarifying questions, show uncertainty, verify calculations and route high-stakes decisions to qualified people.

09 · Safety & limits
Risks, safeguards and failure modes
Safety is a system property that needs ongoing testing, governance and response plans.


Reliability
Hallucination, brittle reasoning, distribution shift, overconfidence, arithmetic errors, inconsistent answers and poor uncertainty estimates.
Security
Prompt injection, data leakage, tool abuse, insecure code output, malicious model artifacts, model theft and adversarial inputs.
Social impact
Bias, privacy violations, surveillance, labor disruption, misinformation, accessibility gaps, concentrated power and unequal access.
Safeguards
Data minimization, access controls, sandboxing, red-teaming, policy enforcement, evaluations, monitoring, human escalation and incident response.
Model extraction & privacy
Repeated queries may reveal behavior or sensitive training information in some settings. Differential privacy, output restrictions and access monitoring can reduce selected risks, with trade-offs.
Responsible use
Do not place sensitive information in a service unless its data handling is understood. Independently verify high-stakes medical, legal, financial, engineering and safety-critical outputs.
Scientific humility: no test suite proves that a model is safe in every situation. Claims should be tied to a defined threat model, evaluation method, operating conditions and evidence.



10 · Deployment
From weights to a production service
Model engineering includes systems around the neural network.


Hardware and performance
GPUs and other accelerators perform large matrix operations in parallel. Performance depends on memory bandwidth, compute throughput, batch size, sequence length, kernel efficiency, interconnects and concurrency.
Training: throughput and stable distributed optimization. Inference: time-to-first-token, tokens per second, memory footprint, tail latency and cost per request.


Serving architecture
Common pieces include request routing, batching, scheduling, KV-cache management, quantization, model sharding, safety filters, observability, rate limits, authentication and fallbacks.
Version control matters: record model hashes, tokenizer, configuration, prompts, dependencies, dataset lineage and evaluation results. A model update should be tested and reversible.


Lifecycle and monitoring
Monitor drift, failures, cost, abuse patterns and user feedback. Define rollback triggers. Re-evaluate after model, prompt, retrieval corpus, tool or policy changes. Keep an audit trail appropriate to privacy and legal requirements.


Model access spectrum
Hosted API: convenient, provider controls internals. Open weights: downloadable parameters under a license, but training data and recipe may still be undisclosed. Open source: a broader claim involving source and licensing; not every “open-weight” release qualifies.
Read licenses and model cards. “Open” does not automatically mean unrestricted, fully reproducible or safe.



The big talk · future intelligence
AGI, ASI and the open questions
These terms describe debated capability concepts, not standardized product grades.


AGI
Artificial General Intelligence
AGI commonly refers to an AI system able to learn, reason and perform a wide range of cognitive tasks at a level comparable to or beyond humans, including adapting to unfamiliar problems. But definitions differ: some emphasize breadth, others autonomy, learning efficiency, economic usefulness or human-level performance across domains.
There is no universally accepted AGI benchmark, test, or agreed declaration threshold. A system can be superhuman in some narrow tasks and weak in others. Broad benchmark performance alone may not establish robust real-world generality.
Questions researchers must answer: Can it reliably transfer skills? Learn new tasks from limited evidence? Plan over long horizons? Notice when it is wrong? Operate safely under distribution shift? Work with the physical world? Maintain performance without extensive human scaffolding?


ASI
Artificial Superintelligence
ASI is a hypothetical system that substantially exceeds the best human capabilities across most or all relevant intellectual domains. Discussions often include accelerated scientific discovery, strategic planning, software engineering and the ability to improve AI research itself.
ASI is not an established current category with a verified example. Its arrival, feasibility, timeline and behavior are uncertain. Forecasts differ widely, and extrapolating from current benchmark curves is not enough to settle the issue.
Why it matters: if a system became far more capable than its operators in consequential domains, mistakes, misuse, concentration of power, loss of control and competitive deployment pressures could have unusually large effects.


Possible technical paths
Researchers discuss several (non-exclusive) routes: continued scaling of foundation models + better data and post-training; stronger reasoning and search at inference time; multi-agent systems; better world models and long-horizon planning; embodiment and robotics; hybrid neuro-symbolic designs; recursive self-improvement of research tools.
None is proven to be sufficient or necessary. Progress on one axis (e.g. language fluency) does not automatically deliver progress on others (e.g. reliable long-term autonomy or physical competence).


Capability is not the same as autonomy
A model may answer difficult questions yet be unable to act independently. Autonomy depends on persistent goals, access to tools, permissions, memory, planning loops, resources and the length of tasks it can complete without intervention. Combining tools and models can create capabilities not present in a single isolated model.
Likewise, autonomy does not automatically mean consciousness or a desire to survive. Those are distinct claims requiring distinct evidence.


Alignment and control
Alignment asks whether a system's behavior reliably reflects human intentions and legitimate constraints. Technical approaches include training objectives, scalable oversight, interpretability, evaluations, sandboxing, monitoring, restricted permissions, staged deployment and shutdown mechanisms.
No single method is a complete solution. A credible safety case combines empirical evidence, adversarial testing, governance, accountability and operational controls proportionate to the system's capability and reach.


Timelines and uncertainty
Expert surveys and public forecasts show wide disagreement—from a few years to many decades or longer, and some argue AGI-as-commonly-imagined may never arrive in the form expected. Base rates from past AI winters, measurement difficulties, and the difference between lab demos and robust real-world systems all argue for humility.
The responsible stance is to track concrete capabilities, invest in measurement and safety research, and update beliefs as evidence changes—not to treat any single timeline as settled fact.


A grounded conclusion
Today's AI systems already provide valuable capabilities, but they also make errors, can be manipulated and vary across tasks. AGI is a disputed target; ASI remains hypothetical. Neither should be treated as inevitable, impossible, or proven by a dramatic demo.
The scientific approach is to define terms, measure capabilities, publish methods, investigate failure modes and update beliefs as evidence changes.


Closing talk: The future of AI is not just a race to build a bigger model. It is a question of what systems can reliably do, who controls them, how their behavior is verified, and whether benefits are shared while risks are constrained. AGI and ASI deserve serious study—but the responsible position is evidence-led curiosity, not hype or panic.



Society, ethics & policy
AI in the world
Technical capability is only one dimension. Who benefits, who is harmed, and who decides matter.


Labor and economy
Automation can raise productivity, create new jobs, and displace existing ones. Effects differ by sector, skill level, and geography. Historical technology shifts offer partial analogies but not precise predictions. Policy options include education, transition support, competition policy, and measurement of real outcomes rather than slogans.


Bias, fairness and representation
Models trained on human data can reflect and amplify existing patterns of bias. Fairness is multi-dimensional (group vs individual, different protected attributes, different error costs). Mitigation includes data work, evaluation, post-processing, and careful deployment choices—none of which is a permanent fix.


Misinformation and influence
Generative systems can lower the cost of producing persuasive text, images, and audio. This can aid education and creativity, and also scale scams, propaganda, and deepfakes. Detection, provenance, platform design, and media literacy are partial responses.


Concentration of power
Training frontier models currently requires large capital, specialized chips, and talent. This can concentrate capability in a small number of organizations and states. Open weights, public compute, independent evaluation, and international coordination are among the responses under discussion.


Governance approaches
Options range from soft norms and model cards, through standards and audits, to licensing or restrictions on certain capabilities. Different jurisdictions are experimenting. Effective governance needs technical literacy, measurable goals, and the ability to adapt as systems change.


Individual responsibility
Users and builders can verify high-stakes claims, protect sensitive data, disclose AI assistance when it matters, and prefer systems with transparent limitations. Not every problem is a model problem; many are process, incentive, and power problems.
